Pricing Short-Term Rentals in Rio

Hedonic pricing · XGBoost · Panel fixed effects · Instrumental variables · Forward demand · Python · 5 tests

The business question

A host in Rio, or a company that manages hundreds of apartments, sets a nightly price with three questions in mind. What is a place like this worth here? If I charge more, how many stays do I lose? And which dates are already filling up? Revenue management in hotels and car rental asks the same questions with internal booking data. This case answers them with public data only.

The data

Inside Airbnb (CC BY 4.0) publishes a scrape of every Rio listing each quarter. This case uses the four scrapes with prices, from 27 November 2025 to 22 September 2026: 44,058 listings of 24,694 hosts in the latest one, and a panel of 41,440 listings seen at least twice. Each listing has one nightly price, quoted for a stay a few days after the scrape. 1.46 million dated reviews are the proxy for stays: a review follows a stay, so reviews in the 90 days after a scrape measure the stays that followed that price.

Two changes in the source shaped the design. The calendar files no longer carry a price per night, only whether the night is still available, so price by date and booking lead time cannot be studied. And the scrape of 28 December 2025 has no prices at all, so it is left out.

Try it

Describe a place and see its estimated market price. Then set your price above or below the market and see what the estimated elasticity implies for stays and revenue. The calendar shows how full each date of the next twelve months already was in September, and the neighbourhood table compares prices and bookings across the city.

Method

What a night is worth. A hedonic model regresses the log nightly price on the neighbourhood, the type of place, guests, bedrooms, bathrooms, rating, number of reviews, amenities, superhost status, instant booking and minimum nights. The log-linear version gives coefficients a host can read and runs in the simulator; XGBoost on the same features shows what a flexible model adds. Both are scored on a random fifth of the listings and, separately, on the listings that appeared for the first time in the latest scrape.

How stays respond to price. For listings seen in several scrapes, the log of one plus the stays in the next 90 days is regressed on the log price, with a fixed effect for each listing and for each neighbourhood in each scrape. The listing effect removes lasting quality, which makes good places both expensive and busy; the neighbourhood-scrape effect removes the season and local demand. What is left is the same host moving the price of the same place, relative to the neighbourhood. Since a host may raise the price when expecting more demand, the price is also instrumented with the mean price of the same host’s other listings in that scrape: a decision taken for the whole portfolio, not for this one place’s demand.

What is already booked. For each of the next 365 nights after 22 September 2026, the share of active listings whose night is no longer available. A night can be unavailable because it was booked or because the host blocked it; the two cannot be told apart.

Results

Location and size set most of the price. The log-linear model explains 54% of the variance of log prices. On a random fifth of listings, its median error is 31% and XGBoost’s 28%. On listings that had just appeared, the errors rise to 55% and 47%: new hosts price far from what comparable places charge, which is where a pricing tool helps most. Ipanema has a median price of R$ 750, Barra da Tijuca R$ 640, Copacabana, with 13,645 listings, R$ 457.

Stays fall little when a host raises the price. Within the same listing, a 10% higher price goes with about 1.6% fewer stays: the coefficient is -0.17 (95% interval -0.18 to -0.16), and measured on stays in levels the elasticity at the mean is -0.24. Instrumented by the host’s other listings, the estimate is the same, -0.17 (interval -0.24 to -0.11, first-stage F of 392), so the hosts’ own expectations do not seem to drive it. At these values a moderate price increase raises revenue: +10% in price, about -1.6% in stays, about +8% in revenue.

New Year’s Eve and Carnival fill early. On 22 September, 67% of the listings were already unavailable for New Year’s Eve, against 23% for the first twenty days of December; the Carnival days of February 2027 were at 61%, against 36% in the weeks after it. Nights more than eight months away also look full (56% from June 2027 on), but that is hosts closing their calendars far ahead, not bookings.

Limits

Reviews undercount stays and do not say how many nights each stay lasted, so the elasticity is about the number of stays, not nights or revenue directly. The price is one quote per scrape, for a date near the scrape, not the price of every night. The panel covers four scrapes in a single year, so the elasticity is a short-run response to moderate price moves; far from the market price the response can be larger. Unavailable nights mix bookings and blocks. Inside Airbnb’s data are a scrape of public pages, not Airbnb’s records.

Applied to your business

This is the Custom Forecasting package applied to price. For a property manager, a hotel, a car rental or any business that sells nights or days of capacity, the same pieces run on your own bookings: a market price for each unit, a price response measured on your history, and a forward view of the dates that are filling up, with booking dates and prices per night where this public source has none.
