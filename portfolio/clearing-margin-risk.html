Margin and Default Resources for a Clearing House

Filtered historical simulation · GARCH · Expected shortfall · Black-76 · Back-testing · Procyclicality · Stress testing · Cover 2 · Liquidation · Model governance · Python · 17 tests

The business question

A clearing house stands between the two sides of every trade it clears. If a member fails, the clearing house still has to pay the other side, so it collects margin from every member every day and keeps a default fund for what margin does not cover. Three questions decide whether that works: is the margin enough most of the time, does it jump in a crisis just when members can least afford it, and could the clearing house survive the failure of its two largest members and close their positions without losing more than it holds?

This case answers the three for a clearing house of bitcoin and ether futures and options, on public market data, and documents the model in a whitepaper written the way a risk committee or a regulator reviews one.

The data

All public, with no account needed:

  • Prices: Coinbase daily closes of BTC and ETH in dollars, from May 2016 to 27 November 2026, 3,843 days.
  • Options and futures: a snapshot of the Deribit market on 27 November 2026, every listed option (1,722) and future (28) with its mark price, implied volatility and open interest, and the full order books of the futures.
  • Implied volatility: Deribit’s DVOL index, daily since March 2024.

Try it

Build a book of futures and options and see its margin computed in your browser on the same scenarios as the model; follow the back-test day by day; compare the procyclicality tools; pick a stress scenario and see which members the default fund has to cover; see what it costs to close a defaulter’s position over different horizons; and see why binary options need a different margin rule near expiry.

Method, and why this one

Margin by filtered historical simulation. Each asset gets a GARCH(1,1) with Student-t errors. Dividing returns by their conditional volatility leaves standardised residuals that keep the dependence between BTC and ETH on the same day. A scenario draws two days of those residuals (the margin period of risk: the time to close a defaulter’s position), scales them by today’s volatility and adds them up. The margin is the 99% expected shortfall of the loss across 20,000 scenarios. This is the method most clearing houses use for futures and options, because it reacts to the market without assuming normal returns.

Options repriced in full. Every option is revalued with Black-76 on each scenario of the price and of implied volatility, so its gamma and vega enter the margin, not only its delta. Each drawn day carries its own change of implied volatility. A SPAN-style grid of 16 price and volatility moves runs alongside as a benchmark.

Back-tested as it would be used. From 2019, the margin is set every other day from data up to that day only and compared with the loss over the next two days: 1,443 non-overlapping windows. Kupiec’s test checks the breach rate, Christoffersen’s checks that breaches do not cluster, and the traffic light counts breaches over the last 250 windows.

Procyclicality measured, then limited. Three anti-procyclicality tools: a 25% buffer released in stress, a floor at half the margin of the most volatile year, and a 25% weight on that stressed margin. Each is scored on how much it damps the swings and what it costs in average margin.

Cover 2 on the real open interest. The live open interest of each Deribit instrument is split at random between ten synthetic members, long and short, so the cleared book nets to zero as a real one does. Under the six worst and six best two-day moves since 2016 and two hypothetical ones, the loss each member’s margin does not cover is what the default fund must absorb. Cover 2 is the sum of the two largest in the worst scenario.

Liquidation on the real order book. The largest defaulter’s futures are sold into the visible bids of the perpetual, at once or in slices over hours, trading the impact of large orders against the risk of holding the rest.

Governance. A model inventory, a change policy with approval levels, monitoring thresholds with amber and red levels and who is told, and CI on every change.

Result

The margin passes its back-test, and the simpler model does not. Over 1,443 windows, the model’s 99% VaR is breached 16 times for long BTC (Kupiec p = 0.68), 20 for long ETH (0.16) and 12 for long BTC against short ETH (0.51), with no clustering. Plain historical simulation breaches less than 1% of the time because its window keeps the 2017 to 2020 crashes long after they pass, so it charges too much in calm years: 14% more margin on average for ETH, and Kupiec rejects it at 5% for ETH and for the spread.

A hedge that is not a hedge. Long US$ 1 million of BTC against short US$ 1 million of ETH needs more margin today (US$ 134 thousand) than the long BTC alone (US$ 122 thousand), although the two move together. ETH is a third more volatile and has its own right tail: its three largest two-day rises were 37% to 47%. A short ETH leg carries that tail.

The responsive model swings, and the tools cost money. For long BTC, the model’s margin moves 9.5 times from its trough to its peak and rose fivefold within 15 days. A 25% weight on the stressed-period margin brings the swing to 5.3 times for 9% more margin on average; a 25% buffer cuts the largest 15-day jump to fourfold for 22% more. Which to use is a decision for the risk committee, and this table is the input it needs.

Cover 2 is US$ 150 million. In the worst scenario, BTC down 40% with implied volatility up 30 points, the two members with the largest net long BTC positions leave US$ 149.6 million uncovered, against US$ 194.3 million of total member margin. The March 2020 move alone gives US$ 142.3 million. Concentration drives the result: two members out of ten carry it.

The book is too thin to close a defaulter at once. The largest member in Cover 2 holds US$ 286 million of net long BTC futures; the visible bids of the perpetual add up to US$ 48 million. Selling over about four hours costs least, about US$ 4 million, under the optimistic assumption that the book refills between slices. Waiting longer adds more price risk than it saves in impact. When the book is this thin, a default auction to other members, after hedging the delta, is the alternative the whitepaper sets out.

Binary options need a different rule near expiry. Priced from the live smile, an at-the-money binary is worth 0.52 per dollar, against 0.47 with a single volatility. A short binary needs 65% of its maximum loss 30 days before expiry, 96% a week before, and the whole maximum loss within four days.

Limits

The data covers one exchange; the options and order books are one snapshot, because a daily history of the volatility surface is not public, so the back-test covers futures portfolios and the option margins are as of today. Members are synthetic: real portfolios are more concentrated in some places and more hedged in others. The liquidation assumes the book refills between slices and ignores what a large seller reveals. DVOL starts in 2024; earlier changes of implied volatility are predicted from returns. No figure here is a margin requirement of any clearing house, and no model has had an independent validation.

Applied to your business

For a clearing house, an exchange, a broker that margins its clients, or a fund that wants to know its margin before the clearing house calls it, the same framework runs on your positions: margin by portfolio, back-tested; the cost and effect of each anti-procyclicality tool; stress tests and default fund sizing; and a liquidation plan for the largest positions. It starts with the Data & AI Diagnostic and runs in your environment.
