Fraud Detection: Cost, Capacity, Graphs and Screening

Gradient boosting · Cost-sensitive threshold · Tree SHAP · PSI · Isolation forest · Autoencoder · GraphSAGE · PageRank · Fuzzy name matching · Python · PyTorch · JavaScript · 7 tests

The business question

A fraud team does not need the model with the best AUC. It needs to know which transactions to stop or review today, how many alerts its analysts can read, what each missed fraud and each needless alert costs, why the model flagged a payment, when the pattern has changed under the model, and whether a new customer’s name is on a sanctions list.

This case answers those questions on public data, in four parts: card fraud, illicit flows in a payment network, detection without labels, and name screening.

The data

All public, downloaded by a script with no account:

  • Card transactions (Université Libre de Bruxelles, Dal Pozzolo et al., 2015): 284,807 European card payments over two days, 492 of them fraud (0.17%). The publisher replaced the original features with 28 principal components; time and amount are kept.
  • The Elliptic payment graph (Weber et al., 2019): 203,769 Bitcoin transactions linked by 234,355 flows over 49 time steps of about two weeks, 4,545 labelled illicit, 42,019 licit and the rest unknown.
  • Sanctions lists: the OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list with its alternate names (US Treasury) and the UN Security Council consolidated list, 43,648 names in all.

Try it

Set the cost of reviewing an alert and move the threshold to see alerts, frauds caught and the total cost; see how many frauds a team of a given size reaches; open the explanation of an alert; watch every model fail after a dark market closed; and screen any name against the sanctions lists. The screening runs in your browser and nothing is sent.

Method, and why this one

A split in time, never at random. Fraud changes, and a model is used on the future. The card data is split by time: the first 60% to train, the next 20% to choose the threshold, the last 20% (about ten hours, 75 frauds) to report. The graph is split as in the original paper: train on time steps 1 to 34, test on 35 to 49.

A threshold chosen by cost, not by 0.5. A missed fraud costs its amount; every alert costs a review and some friction with a legitimate customer. The threshold minimises the sum on the validation period and is then applied, unchanged, to the test period (Bahnsen, Aouada and Ottersten, 2014). The models are logistic regression and gradient boosting (Chen and Guestrin, 2016); ranking quality is reported as the area under the precision-recall curve, which is what matters at 0.17% fraud.

A queue sized to the team. When the team can read k alerts an hour, the question is not the threshold but the order: the k highest scores of each hour go to review.

Every alert explained. Gradient boosting gives exact SHAP contributions per tree path (Lundberg et al., 2020): how much each feature pushed this payment’s score up or down. The population stability index tracks each feature and the score between training and today.

Graphs, where the money moves. On Elliptic, random forests on the node’s own features, on features aggregated from its neighbours, and on features from the network itself (in and out degree, PageRank, neighbour means), against GraphSAGE (Hamilton, Ying and Leskovec, 2017), two layers of mean aggregation written in plain PyTorch on a sparse adjacency matrix.

Without labels. New fraud patterns have no labels yet. Isolation forest (Liu, Ting and Zhou, 2008) and an autoencoder learn only what a normal transaction looks like, and are then scored against the labels they never saw.

Screening that tolerates spelling. Names are normalised (accents, punctuation, case), transliteration variants are folded (KH and H, Y and I, W and V and others), and compared with a token-sort and a token-set similarity. Token-set similarity is only used when both names have two or more words: otherwise a one-word alias such as “DANIEL” would flag every customer called Daniel. The same code runs in the page and gives the same scores as the Python, checked in CI.

Result

The cost-based threshold cuts the cost of fraud by almost two thirds. With US$ 5 per review, gradient boosting alerts on 209 of the 56,962 test payments and catches 62 of the 75 frauds; the cost of missed fraud plus reviews is US$ 2,796, against US$ 7,729 with no model. Its threshold is 0.02, far from 0.5. Logistic regression, at its own best threshold, catches 50 with 52 alerts and costs US$ 3,325. Gradient boosting also ranks better: PR-AUC 0.80 against 0.75, while both have a ROC-AUC of 0.98, which hides the difference.

The threshold follows the cost of a review. At US$ 10 per alert it jumps to 0.84: 62 alerts, 56 frauds caught, 90% precision. At US$ 50 every one of the 45 alerts is fraud and 30 frauds get through. That is a business decision the model supports, not one it makes.

Team size has a sharp limit. Reading the 5 highest scores of each hour reaches 31 of the 75 frauds; 10 an hour, 49; 20 an hour, 60; 50 an hour, only 65.

Without labels, half as good. In its strangest 1% of payments, isolation forest finds 51% of the frauds and the autoencoder 35%, against 87% for gradient boosting. Their PR-AUC is 0.05 and 0.03 against a base rate of 0.13%: useful to surface new patterns for analysts, not to replace a supervised model.

A pattern change breaks every model. On the payment graph, a random forest with all features reaches an illicit F1 of 0.82 (precision 0.95, recall 0.72); features from the network add a little to the node’s own; GraphSAGE reaches 0.62, in line with the original paper, where random forests also beat graph networks on this data. But F1 is 0.91 up to time step 42 and 0.03 after: a dark market closed, the illicit flows changed, and no model trained before saw the new pattern. The answer is monitoring and retraining, not a deeper model.

Screening at similarity 88: every altered name found, no false alert. On 300 listed names with one change (order, transliteration, a missing letter, a dropped middle name, accents) and 300 common Brazilian and English names, a threshold of 88 finds all 300 and alerts on none. At 80, 3% of common names alert; at 75, 17%. Requiring a perfect score would miss 15% of the altered names.

Limits

The card data covers two days and its features are anonymised, so the explanations show the mechanism, not the business meaning, and the cost figures use the transaction amount as the loss, without chargebacks, recoveries or the value of a lost customer. The hour of day shifts between training and test by construction. Labels on the graph are incomplete: 77% of its nodes are unknown. The screening test uses altered names and synthetic common names, not a real customer base; a production system adds dates of birth, nationalities and addresses to clear alerts. There is no streaming or real-time component here: the models are batch, as in the first months of most fraud programmes.

Applied to your business

For a bank, a payment institution or a marketplace, the same steps run on your transactions, chargebacks and alert outcomes: a threshold set by your costs, a queue sized to your team, an explanation for every alert, a drift report every week, and screening against the lists you are required to check. It starts with the Data & AI Diagnostic and runs in your environment.
