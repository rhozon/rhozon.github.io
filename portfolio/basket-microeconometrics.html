Market Basket Analysis with Microeconometrics

Association rules · Fixed effects · Leave-one-out instruments · Multiple testing · Basket completion · Python and R · 6 tests

The business question

Every retailer has a table of products bought together, and the pairs with the highest lift end up in promotions: discount the pasta sauce, the pasta will follow. The table cannot say whether that is true. Two products appear together for three different reasons: they are used together (complements), big shopping trips contain a bit of everything, and the same households like both. Only the first one means that a discount on one sells more of the other. This case separates the three, tests whether price and promotions move complements at all, and shows where lift is still useful.

The data

The Complete Journey from 84.51°, the data science company of the Kroger group, distributed in the completejourney R package (CC0): a year of purchases of 2,469 households, 155,848 shopping trips, 1.47 million lines in 457 stores, with the price paid, the loyalty-card discount and the coupon of every line, and 20.9 million weekly records of which products were on display or in the weekly mailer in each store. The analysis uses the 46 most frequent grocery categories, from soft drinks and milk to pasta, pasta sauce, hot dogs and peanut butter. The data are downloaded by an R script and analysed in Python.

Try it

Pick a category to see its partners under three measures; give a category a deeper discount and see the estimated effect on everything else, with its uncertainty; or tick what is already in a basket and compare what lift and the adjusted association would suggest.

Method

Classic basket analysis first. Support, confidence and lift for every pair of the 46 categories.

Then what lift is made of. Lift is recomputed within deciles of trip size, which removes the pull of big trips. A second measure removes each household’s average purchase of each category and the trip size, and correlates what is left: co-purchase that is neither taste nor trip size, the part closest to use in the same meal (Gentzkow, 2007, makes the same distinction between complementarity and correlated tastes).

Do promotions move complements? For each category, the chance that it is in a basket is regressed on every category’s marketing in that store and week: the depth of the loyalty-card discount, the number of products on display and in the weekly mailer. Household fixed effects absorb lasting tastes; month and holiday effects absorb the calendar. The discount each basket faced is measured on the other baskets of the same store and week, so a shopper’s own purchases never enter the price they face. With 46 categories there are 2,070 cross effects for each kind of marketing, so the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controls the share of false discoveries. A second specification uses the price paid relative to each product’s usual price.

Where lift still helps. A basket-completion test hides each category of each basket in turn and asks which model ranks it highest: popularity, the household’s habit, habit plus lift, habit plus the adjusted association. Associations come from the first 26 weeks, the weight is chosen on weeks 27 to 39, and the comparison is made on weeks 40 to 53.

Results

Lift is mostly trip size. Every one of the 1,035 pairs has a lift above one, with a mean of 2.53: everything seems to go with everything. Within trips of similar size the mean falls to 1.27. Pasta and pasta sauce stay at the top under every measure (raw lift 10.9, 4.4 net of trip size, a residual correlation of 0.42), but the rankings move: of the 50 pairs with the highest lift, only 22 are among the 50 with the highest adjusted association.

Promotions move their own category a little, and nothing else measurably. A 10-point deeper discount on milk raises the chance that milk is in the basket by 0.66 points, on a base of 25%; for most categories the own effect is smaller and not distinguishable from zero. Across the 46 categories, 7 own discount effects are positive at the 5% level and 4 survive the false-discovery control; for displays, 12 and 5; for mailers, 8 and 2. None of the 2,070 cross effects survives, for discounts, displays or mailers. Between 6% and 9% of them are significant at the 5% level, against 5% by chance alone, none by enough to stand out.

The upper bounds are the useful part. A 10-point deeper discount on pasta sauce changes the chance of buying pasta by 0.006 points; the 95% interval reaches 0.11 points, on a base of 4.1%. For hot dogs and buns, cereal and milk, chips and soft drinks, the upper bounds stay below 0.4 points. In this panel, a halo of half a point or more would have been seen.

For prediction, lift works. Out of sample, household habit alone reaches an AUC of 0.764 in the basket-completion test, popularity 0.654. Adding lift raises it to 0.810, the adjusted association to 0.803. Lift predicts what else is in the basket well, because it carries the size of the trip; it does not say what a promotion will move.

Limits

One retailer, one year, 2,469 loyalty-card households: the result is about this panel. Marketing is measured at the category level; a promotion on one brand dilutes into the category’s average, which weakens own effects and can hide product-level halos. Discounts are chosen by the retailer, and the fixed effects and leave-one-out measurement remove the main confounders but do not make them random. The adjusted association is closer to joint use than lift, but it is a correlation, not a causal effect. The way to settle a specific halo is a randomized promotion, and the intervals above give its size.

Applied to your business

For a retailer, a distributor or a consumer-goods company, the same pipeline runs on your transactions: what lift really measures in your stores, which promotions move their own category and which move nothing else, the size of the test that would settle a halo, and a basket recommender for the app or the sales team.
